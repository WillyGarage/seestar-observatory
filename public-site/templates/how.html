A backyard telescope that runs itself

Every picture here comes from one small smart telescope on a roof in Sunnyvale. On clear nights an AI runs it on its own. It picks what to shoot, waits for the right moment, aims, keeps an eye on the weather, turns the raw shots into a finished picture and posts it here. Nobody sits at an eyepiece.

Anyone can ask for something to be photographed. The software behind it all is open source on GitHub.

Why lots of short shots instead of a few long ones?

The telescope never holds perfectly still on the sky, so short shots come out sharper.

  • The view slowly turns. This telescope swivels and tilts like a camera on an ordinary tripod. Big observatory telescopes are tipped to line up with the Earth's axis. It can keep an object centred, but over time the picture rotates around it. In 10 seconds that doesn't show. Over 30 seconds, stars near the edges start to smear.
  • Small wobbles add up. Wind and tiny motor errors blur a long shot more than a short one.
  • Bad luck costs less. A plane crossing the view spoils 10 seconds, not a whole minute.
  • Stacking makes up the difference. Because every shot gets lined up again before it's added, the drift between shots doesn't matter. Sixty-seven 10-second shots come out almost as clean as twenty-two 30-second ones.

What makes a picture better is more total time. The Great Pegasus Cluster used 471 shots, 78 minutes of light, and it's the best picture here so far.
